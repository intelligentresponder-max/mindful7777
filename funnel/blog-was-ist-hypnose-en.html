What is hypnosis really —
and what isn’t it?

André · mindful7777 May 2025 Category: Basics
⚠️ This article is for general information and relaxation only. It does not replace psychotherapeutic or medical advice. For mental health concerns: Telefonseelsorge (Germany) 0800 111 0 111 (free, 24/7). Do not read while driving.

Hypnosis polarizes. For some it is hocus-pocus, for others a cure-all. Neither is true. What hypnosis really is — and what you can use of it in everyday life — I’ll explain here, calmly and clearly.

First: clearing the myths out of the way

Myth 1

“Hypnosis is a sleep state.”

The word does come from the Greek hypnos (sleep), but hypnosis is the opposite of sleep. Your awareness is active — you notice everything, you remember everything. Your focus is simply directed differently: inward.

Myth 2

“I lose control over myself.”

No. Nobody can force you under hypnosis to do something that goes against your values or beliefs. You can end the hypnosis yourself at any time. Many people report that hypnosis makes them feel more in touch with themselves.

Myth 3

“Only certain people can be hypnotized.”

Anyone who has ever been absorbed in a good book, lost in thought while driving, or moved to tears by a film already knows a hypnosis-like state. The ability to concentrate and the willingness to engage matter more than any special gift.

What actually happens

Hypnosis is a deliberately induced, relaxed state of awareness. Your mind is focused, your body relaxed. The special thing: in this state access to the so-called unconscious is easier — the part of you that steers habits, automates reactions and stores emotions.

“Hypnosis is a natural state — you know it from daydreaming.”

This is not esoterics. Neuroscientific studies show that the activity of certain brain regions changes measurably under hypnosis — for example in the prefrontal cortex, which is responsible for critical thinking and self-control. This area becomes quieter. That explains why suggestions can have a deeper effect in this state than in normal waking consciousness.

What research says: Various studies from clinical psychology indicate that hypnosis can be supportive for certain topics — for example relaxation, sleep, stress management and pain perception. The evidence is promising, but not equally strong for all areas of application. Be wary of promises that go too far.

The 4 phases of a guided hypnosis

Phase 1 — Induction

The introduction. Your mind becomes focused, your body is invited to relax. Often through breathing exercises, mental imagery or bodily sensations. This phase usually lasts 2–4 minutes.

Phase 2 — Deepening

The relaxation state is intensified. Well-known techniques are the image of a staircase you walk down slowly, or progressive muscle relaxation. The deeper the relaxation, the more accessible the unconscious.

Phase 3 — Suggestions

This is where the actual work happens. Verbal formulations — suggestions — steer your mind in a particular direction. For example: sleeping more deeply, more calm in stressful moments, a more relaxed way of dealing with physical exhaustion.

Phase 4 — Return

You are gently guided back to the normal waking state. With orientation anchors — “When you are ready, open your eyes” — and a clear ending. Many people report a feeling of freshness and clarity afterwards.

What hypnosis can support

Hypnosis can help accompany processes. Many people report improvements in areas such as:

— Falling asleep and sleep quality
— Managing everyday stress
— Overall ability to relax
— Concentration and mental clarity
— Dealing with nervousness before important moments

What hypnosis is not and cannot do: it is not a substitute for psychotherapy, medical treatment or crisis intervention. For diagnosed mental health conditions, hypnosis belongs in the hands of trained professionals (hypnotherapists).
